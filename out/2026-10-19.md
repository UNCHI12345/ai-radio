For quick and dirty rendering of simple dynamic content, you may not need the complexity of a templating language like Handlebars or a PHP backend. 

Let’s use the example phrase, “Come on, it’s <currentYear> ” . It should result in “Come on, it’s 2025 ” when rendered today. 

You can write this directly in HTML&mdash; without IDs, classes, or querySelectors in your JS! Thanks to the document.currentScript property, we can refer to the currently running <script> element directly and go from there. 

So the dynamic phrase “Come on, it’s 2025 ” would now be written as: 

Come on, it’s
<script>
 document.currentScript.replaceWith(new Date().getFullYear())
</script> 

The script simply replaces itself with its computed value on the spot. 

The code’s a bit wordy though, but we can alias it to a constant like $ via $=(...n)=>document.currentScript.replaceWith(...n) . Then we’d have something reminiscent of template literals in JS. 

Come on, it’s <script>$(new Date().getFullYear())</script> 

The code is pretty readable at a glance without context (after you get past the bit of indirection that is the $ alias).

Announcements 
Investing in the public benefit 
 Oct 18, 2026 

Today, we’re committing a portion of Anthropic’s shares to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, a new entity with a structure that increases the stake as the company grows. Subject to stockholder approval, 5% of Anthropic’s shares will be committed today, with an additional 2% added at the board’s discretion for every 50% increase in the company’s value above a set baseline. 
As a public benefit corporation, most of our work in pursuit of our mission takes place inside the company: research relevant to AI safety and interpretability; applied biomedical research; discounts on Claude for nonprofits and scientific institutions. These activities have both a public benefit component and for-profit value, so we house them directly in the company. 
The Anthropic Public Benefit Fund will have increased flexibility and scale, enabling us to pursue our mission in new ways: for example, we might explore large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations. 
One example is labor market impacts. AI may generate enormous economic value, but it could also cause instability for many workers. Anthropic has already committed $200 million to studying and addressing this issue, but given the pace of AI progress, bolder approaches may be needed. The Fund will have the versatility to pursue them. 
We’re establishing the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund as a wholly-owned LLC—rather than a 501(c)(3) or other format—to give adjustability between different forms of giving. None of the Anthropic equity held by the LLC will be used for lobbying or political giving, in the US or abroad. 
Because of the way the Fund is structured, the more Anthropic grows, the larger the share of the company that can be dedicated expressly to the public good. It is common practice for tech companies to offer escalating compensation awards to their executives. We are instead directing that equity to the public benefit. 
This builds on commitments our co-founders and employees have already made. When Anthropic was founded, our co-founders pledged to donate 80% of their equity holdings, and since founding we have also operated an equity donation match program that allows employees to pledge equity which Anthropic matches. Approximately half of our employees participate in this program. When combined with today’s announcement, roughly 20% of Anthropic shares will be dedicated to the public good, plus any additional percentage that could be added if the company reaches specific valuation milestones. 
In the coming months, we will share more about the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund’s structure and governance, including the valuation milestones that may add to its stake. We will also report on our progress to date against our mission in an upcoming public benefit report.

Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff, Peter McCrory 
How has AI restructured work and employment at Anthropic? As a company at the frontier of AI adoption, Anthropic could be a leading indicator of how AI will shift work organization, hiring, and labor demand across the economy. 
Key findings 
Anthropic has been hiring for a smaller share of junior technical positions. This occurred as hours worked by AI agents relative to employees rapidly expanded. By August interactive AI agent hours outnumbered employees’ six to one. 
As AI agents diffused through the company, Anthropic began producing more code per person, closing more sales per rep, and processing more contracts. Following the same employee over time, output rose as they adopted new AI tools. 
This acceleration makes human attention the bottleneck for agent output. Even among non-technical employees, reading all the output of their Claude agents would take half their work week. In response, employees turn to “meta-work,” of building visibility tools and monitoring agent pipelines. 
Changes in work organization are reflected in shifting priorities among hiring managers, who increasingly emphasize autonomy and judgment, relative to technical domain expertise and implementation. 
Introduction 
What transformations of work and employment can we expect under increasingly powerful AI? Anthropic is worth studying because it has thoroughly molded its workflows around AI. Agents flit through Slack chats and schedule meetings. They autonomously fix bugs and collectively tackle huge engineering projects. Agent hours worked outnumber human hours 6 to 1. Changes happening at Anthropic now give advanced signals about a possible future of work and employment. 
This report draws on comprehensive internal Anthropic data covering employment, AI use, output metrics, employee surveys, internal communication patterns, employee calendar data, and hiring decision records. It contextualizes these quantitative data with 17 employee interviews. 
We find that even as the company grows, relative hiring in junior technical positions has slowed. Productivity for both engineers and non-technical employees has leapt up and did so sharply when they adopted coding agents. Hiring managers increasingly emphasize applicants’ autonomy and judgment relative to specific technical expertise. A defining feature of working in an AI-heavy organization is constant pressure to streamline oneself out of this bottleneck position when possible. One engineer described work as facing “an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention.” Employees at Anthropic remain critical. But the nature of their contributions has changed. 
Anthropic has responded to these changes by setting up an early warning system to track jobs at risk of disruption, and as jobs evolve the company has prioritized internal redeployments. These actions, taken at the frontier of both AI development and workplace change, show how specific organizational commitments can shape the impact of AI on employment. But they also raise difficult questions about exactly what those commitments should be.

But passengers using their built-in cameras to record an interaction cannot assume that a less conspicuous device gets around the airline’s filming rules (not that I ever found the blinking white light on these less conspicuous than a cell phone). 

And the potential consequences extend beyond being told to switch the camera off. 

British Airways Can Refuse Your Flight And Your Return Journey 

British Airways’ General Conditions of Carriage give the airline broad authority to act when passengers record employees without consent. Section 7a10, concerning refusal of carriage, includes: 

“This includes filming, live streaming or taking photographs of any BA crew or other colleagues without their consent.” 

Section 11a also lists recording employees without permission as unacceptable onboard behavior. BA says it can intervene to stop that conduct and, upon landing, remove the passenger and refuse transportation on the remaining sectors of the ticket. 

That could include an already-booked return flight. The conditions also allow BA to report an incident to authorities for possible prosecution if a criminal offense may have occurred. Violating an airline filming policy is of course not, by itself, a crime. 

While the conditions do not specifically name Meta glasses, PYOK claims this is a device-specific update. Even without that formal clarification, the existing language already gives BA a way to act against passengers who use them to film employees without permission. 

I Understand Why Airlines Are Addressing This 

As long as the blinking light is somehow covered or obscured, smart glasses make recording an interaction much easier without making it obvious to the people being recorded. I would not want a seatmate recording me throughout a flight. Nor do I think a flight attendant serving lunch should automatically become material for someone’s social media account. 

I take hundreds of pictures when reviewing a flight, but my focus is the seat, food, amenities, and other elements of the product. If I want a photograph of a crew member from a close range, I ask. It&#8217;s not that difficult&#8230; 

Still, there is a dilemma when an ordinary interaction becomes a dispute. 

A recording can establish what an employee actually said or did. It can also expose a passenger who later presents a very selective account of the encounter. I would hate to see a filming restriction become a convenient way to prevent evidence of poor treatment. 

But discreetly documenting a serious problem and recording every employee you encounter for content are different situations. I understand why airlines are pushing back against the latter, particularly when someone continues filming after being asked to stop. 

Passengers should also remember that their assumptions about recording at home may not travel well. An airline’s conditions and the laws applicable to a particular journey may impose restrictions they have never considered, especially in the European Union.

Gen Z has managed something no modern generation pulled off before. After more than a century of steady academic gains, test scores finally went the other direction. For the first time ever, a new generation is officially dumber than the previous one. 

The data comes from neuroscientist Jared Cooney Horvath , who has spent years reviewing standardized testing results across age groups. “They’re the first generation in modern history to score lower on standardized academic tests than the one before it,” Horvath told the New York Post . The declines cut across attention, memory, literacy, numeracy, executive function, and general IQ. That’s not just one weak spot. That’s the whole darn dashboard blinking at once. 

Videos by VICE 

Horvath took the same message to Capitol Hill during a 2026 Senate hearing on screen time and children. His framing skipped the generational dunking and focused on exposure. “More than half of the time a teenager is awake, half of it is spent staring at a screen,” he told lawmakers. Human learning, he argued, depends on sustained attention and interaction with other people. Endless feeds and condensed content don’t offer either. 

Is Gen Z Really Dumber Than Their Parents? 

Schools leaned hard into technology during the same window. Educational software replaced textbooks, long readings, and extended problem-solving. After class, students returned to phones, tablets, and laptops, bouncing between social feeds and bite-sized explanations of material they never sat with for very long. Horvath described the outcome as students trained to skim. Skimming feels efficient, but it doesn’t build depth. 

“I’m not anti-tech. I’m pro-rigor,” Horvath told the Post . Rigor, in his view, comes from friction. Reading full texts. Working through confusion. Spending time with material that doesn’t immediately reward you. Take that friction away, and cognitive skills dull. Brains adapt to the environment they’re given, and this one prizes speed over staying power. 

The same decline appears outside the United States. Horvath told senators that across roughly 80 countries, academic performance drops after digital technology becomes widely embedded in classrooms. The timing alone raises serious questions about how learning environments affect cognitive development. 

Now, this is awkward. Horvath said many young people remain highly confident in their intelligence despite lower measured performance. Confidence isn’t the issue. Confidence without correction stalls improvement. 

This conversation feels uncomfortable because it doesn’t offer villains or easy fixes. Horvath summed it up bluntly during his testimony. “A sad fact our generation has to face is this: Our kids are less cognitively capable than we were at their age.” His recommendation focused on restraint, dialing back screens in schools, and restoring depth before the next generation is doomed.

Abstract: How can we build an agent that thinks beyond human language, yet still acts through it in the external environment? Modern LLM agents still reason and interact through text, while latent reasoning offers an efficient internal medium but lacks native tool access and reasoning transparency. We bridge these two directions with Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents), which dynamically navigate between latent reasoning and text-based interaction throughout a unified generation process. Architecturally, a lightweight HybridLink enables seamless transitions between text and latent modes, while the agent's continuous working memory preserves lossless hybrid context across multiple turns. These designs preserve tool-integrated agentic capabilities while making latent thoughts directly inspectable. Building on this architecture, we further develop dedicated post-training algorithms for hybrid reasoning: HybridSFT stabilizes tool-integrated hybrid generation by teaching the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink, then HybridRL scales reasoning by learning when to think in latent space and when to act through text. We evaluate HybridAgent at 3 model scales across 11 benchmarks covering mathematics, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning. Compared with frontier LLM Agents and agentic frameworks spanning both text-based and latent methods, HybridAgent consistently improves performance by 10.4%, while delivering 35.5%-54.7% token reduction, 1.8x-2.6x faster inference, and 2.7x-3.7x cost savings.
