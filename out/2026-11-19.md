Predicting end-to-end fine-tuning time on shared GPU clusters, from 272 production runs. 

A preview of our upcoming paper, Predicting End-to-End Fine-Tuning Time for Generative Language Models . The full version will be on arXiv soon. 

GPUs are among the most expensive and scarcest resources in AI today. Anyone running a shared cluster has to decide, every day, which jobs go on which GPUs and in what order. That plan is only as good as the estimate of how long each job will take. When a job finishes hours early, GPUs sit idle until the next one is dispatched. When it runs hours late, everything queued behind it slips, experiments wait and delivery dates move. 

Better duration estimates turn this into planning instead of guesswork: what runs, when, and on how many GPUs, with less idle capacity in between. Yet the estimate most teams use is a back-of-the-envelope FLOP calculation. It says nine hours, and the job finishes the next afternoon. 

We wanted to know how far off that math really is, and whether we could do better before a job is even dispatched. So we took 272 real supervised fine-tuning (SFT) jobs from our production pool of NVIDIA H200 GPUs, about 7,382 GPU-hours in total, and compared analytical estimates against learned predictors. 

The short version: the usual FLOP-based estimate explains little of what we observed (R² = 0.31, MAE 12.41 h). A CatBoost regressor that only sees information available before dispatch reaches R² = 0.98, with a mean absolute error of 1.71 h and a median error of 0.51 h in 5-fold cross-validation. The gap comes from two things a closed-form estimate cannot do. The regressor sees more training-specific signals, such as evaluation cadence, checkpoint policy, per-GPU load and fine-tuning regime, and it learns the nonlinear interactions between them from past runs. 

The estimate everyone uses is compute-only

For a dense Transformer, training costs roughly 6·P·D floating-point operations, where P is the parameter count and D the number of training tokens. Divide that by what your GPUs can deliver and you get a training-loop time: 

T_theoretical = (6 · P · D) / (N_GPU · FLOPS_peak · MFU)

Here N_GPU is the number of GPUs, FLOPS_peak is the peak throughput of one GPU from its spec sheet, and MFU (Model FLOPs Utilization) is the fraction of that peak the training actually achieves. Real jobs never run at peak because of memory traffic, communication and kernel inefficiencies, so MFU typically sits somewhere between 30% and 60%, and the right value for a given job is not known in advance. 

This is a good description of the optimization loop. A job is more than its loop, though. Before the first step there is environment setup, model download and loading, and tokenization. During training there are periodic evaluations and checkpoint uploads. At the end, the final model goes to object storage. On a multi-tenant cluster, all of it competes with other jobs for network and storage I/O. 

These overheads depend on configuration, and they do not scale neatly with workload or GPU count. Two jobs with the same nominal compute can finish hours apart. 

To keep the comparison fair, the FLOP baseline we test counts the scheduled evaluation tokens as well as the training tokens, and we sweep MFU over a wide range.

How it's built

Rumble-2-Flash uses the Qwen3-ASR architecture for day-0 support. If your stack runs Transformers or vLLM, it runs Rumble-2-Flash, with no forked runtime or trust_remote_code . 

To find out how small a model could get before accuracy dropped off, we distilled Rumble-2, our larger production model, into it with on-policy distillation. The student doesn't train on transcripts the teacher wrote. It transcribes the audio itself, and the teacher grades that transcript token by token: at every position, it gives its own probability for what should come next, and the student learns to match it. Because the student learns from its own outputs, it gets corrected on exactly the mistakes it makes, with feedback on every token instead of one score per clip. 

On-policy distillation normally requires teacher and student to share a tokenizer, and ours don't: choosing Qwen3-ASR meant taking its vocabulary too. GOLD removes that restriction. It merges tokens where the two tokenizers split the text differently, so both models are compared over the same text. Tokens that exist in both vocabularies are compared directly, and the rest by their sorted probabilities.

Abstract 

Looping is a powerful inductive bias for reasoning. By repeatedly iterating a recurrent block, looped models can scale their effective depth and enable multi-step reasoning in latent space. However, existing looped models are not inherently guided by an explicit measure of progress, making it difficult to determine when to stop looping and how to improve upon the current state. To enable this, we introduce Energy-Based Reasoners ( EBRs ), which learn an energy function over latent reasoning states and formulate looped updates as gradient descent on this energy. The energy scores latent states, while its gradient guides refinement and provides a signal for stopping. To train EBRs effectively, we further introduce exploration-based training to discover additional modes and to better shape the energy landscape over a broader set of states. Across four algorithmic reasoning benchmarks, EBRs match or outperform prior recurrent reasoning models, achieving 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme while using 345&times; less inference compute than the next-best model and maintaining comparable training compute. On all multi-solution benchmarks, EBRs achieve state-of-the-art coverage of distinct valid solutions while maintaining near-100% single-sample accuracy. These results suggest that energy-based models are a promising approach for strong reasoning performance.

We are releasing BF16 , FP8 , and NVFP4 checkpoints for everyone to try and build on. In this post, we explain how carefully designed tasks and efficient infrastructure made these gains possible on a limited compute budget. 
DaisyRose-27B scores higher than the original Qwen3.8-27B model on every benchmark above 1 . The largest gain is on Terminal Bench 4.1, a notoriously hard long-horizon benchmark. Our magic lies in the following three key ingredients: high-quality training data, efficient training backend, and robust sandboxes. 
High-quality Training Data 
We built the tasks in partnership with Eigent AI , starting from Eigent AI’s large pool of tasks in machine learning, software engineering, science, and other fields. Together we built thousands of candidates. About 1,200 of them made it into the final training set. The training tasks are selected based on three criteria. 
 Each task targets a mistake the original model makes. We ran the original Qwen3.8-27B on every training task and carefully redesigned each one so that it brings out the mistakes we want the model to stop making. We then tuned each task until its solve rate fell within a suitable range, neither too easy nor too hard for the model to learn from. 
 Every task is a long job with several problems chained together. Each task entails a chain of interdependent problems, so an agent has to navigate through multiple stages of reasoning, planning, and execution. Each attempt gets up to 1.5 hours in the sandbox, with more than 100 tool-calls and more than 256k output tokens. 
 There is no room for reward hacking. We reviewed every task along with the model's attempts at it. Any task that could be passed without really solving it, or that had a design flaw, was fixed or removed before training. 
Efficient training backend 
RadixArk trained DaisyRose-27B with Miles, our open-source reinforcement learning framework for large-scale model post-training, in fully asynchronous mode: the agent keeps working while the model learns, and neither waits for the other. While 128 H200s run the inference on agent tasks, 16 more run the trainer and learn from the trajectories. After every step, the updated model goes out to the 128 GPUs that run the agent. The learning runs on our internal training backend, which is built for agent training: each step learns from a handful of very long sessions, some as long as 1M tokens, folded into multiple trajectories via harness summarization.
