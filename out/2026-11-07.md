Robots are learning to fold laundry, sort parts, and handle unfamiliar objects. But those demonstrations are often rather narrow and don't explain what a robot has learned or why it fails. 

To study a complex system, scientists often begin with a small, controlled environment, such as a petri dish or terrarium, where they can vary conditions and observe the effects. Robotics needs environments that make this kind of investigation possible—researchers should be able to change the training data or model design, repeat a test, and trace the cause of a failure. 

Today we're introducing a framework for creating these environments; we call them FoundationWorlds. Each pairs a controlled, simulated world targeted at studying robotics in a specific environment with tools for automatically generating tasks and training demonstrations, as well as metrics for measuring a robot's behavior over time. Together, they provide a setting for studying questions such as how models improve as more training data becomes available, and how to best combine high-level reasoning with precise physical control. 

Our first foundation world is ToyBlocks : an open simulation framework built around a robot arm and blocks on a tabletop. The setting is deliberately simple, but the robot must still reason about how the blocks interact and how to arrange them while pushing, flipping, and stacking. 

ToyBlocks automatically generates demonstrations, which are recordings of a robot completing tasks in simulation, without requiring time-consuming teleoperation, in which a person remotely controls the robot. Researchers can easily vary these training experiences. For example, a team could generate more pushing or flipping demonstrations, then evaluate performance on tasks kept out of training. 

Starting with ToyBlocks as our first FoundationWorld, we aim to extend the approach to increasingly complex environments. 

How ToyBlocks works

Building on our earlier work in open robot learning, including MolmoAct 2 , MolmoBot , and MolmoSpaces , ToyBlocks gives researchers a way to study robotics questions in a deliberately constrained domain. 

As a FoundationWorld, ToyBlocks is designed around four requirements: (1) realistic physics and appearance, (2) automatic generation of tasks and demonstrations at scale, (3) challenges that combine reasoning with dexterity, and (4) broad coverage of the tasks possible within a domain. 

Open-ended domains require modeling a wide range of objects and interactions, making it infeasible to fulfill all these requirements. ToyBlocks’ narrow scope, on the other hand, makes it possible to fulfill them all. 

Within ToyBlocks' smaller setting, researchers can work toward investigating how a robot can learn to perform any reasonable task while being confident that the simulated arm and blocks behave very similarly to their physical counterparts. 

A physical robot gripper above wooden blocks in the ToyBlocks setup. 

ToyBlocks is not just about picking and stacking blocks: pictures and letters on the blocks' faces allow for searching, sorting, and arranging tasks. A robot may need to turn a block to see a hidden face before deciding where it belongs. Domain randomization – varying block size, table height, and other physical properties from scene to scene – tests how well models generalize as the geometry changes.

We wanted to test whether LLMs and decision models, specifically Jev and Luna, could solve a hard problem that we encounter regularly (grading identity resolution results). We found that while both models performed okay out of the box (88.8–97.3% accuracy), implementing a hybrid model that combines Jev + Opus achieves frontier accuracy at > 4x lower costs.

What is identity resolution? 

Identity resolution is the process of mapping data to corresponding real people. More precisely, say you have a set of data records D . Determine a set of identities I and mapping m(D)->I such that each record d ∈ D maps to an identity i ∈ I , each i corresponds to exactly one real person in the real world, and no two i correspond to the same person in the real world. 

This is foundational for any company wishing to do outreach, personalization, or analytics on customers. You can’t know, for instance, the lifetime value of a customer without being able to map all their purchases to a single identity. 

For example, the input data might contain the following records. IDR would ideally create one identity, Alice , and associate all of these records with Alice .

The most common and problematic issue with IDR is over-merging . This is when an identity i incorrectly corresponds to multiple people in the real world.

This makes it very difficult to assess the accuracy of any IDR algorithm. In practice, teams will spend months attempting to determine if their IDR is working.

I struggle to believe at an intuitive level that autonomous R&D is so ingenious. I have been monitoring a few side projects: 
 Building a new SOTA engine for Scrabble (current SOTA is not really a neural-network method and doesn't beat the strongest human player) 
 Building a bot to play Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe 
 Researching philosophy and formalizing some proofs for the AI Philosophy contest 
 In all of these cases, the model's judgment is just very poor in a basic way. For example: 
 When reviewing literature, it fails to identify the specific papers or blog posts that are obviously the most important and useful. 
 It will conduct experiments that have no information value because the amount of training data used was obviously too small to distinguish the architectures to be compared. 
 In the philosophy project, where it built a wiki over many tokens and investigations, it kept going down directions which were extremely trivial and had little value. 
 For Ultimate Tic-Tac-Toe, I saw a very clever idea in a blog post that I doubt Astra would ever have come up with; it actually located the blog post but didn't realize its importance or relevance (which could be discerned easily from his strong performance in an online arena and the recency of the post) over papers from previous years that are obviously less relevant. 
 I judged an Apart hackathon last weekend and reviewed eight papers, all of which scored between 60% and 95% on Pangram. Predictably, the papers were often very similar to each other, and kind of methodologically incomplete in a vague but similar way, where they seem to overlook the most straightforward analysis. 
 Maybe the important exception is certain hill-climbing tasks with fast feedback loops.  There is also the fact that Astra at least (and presumably Claude) tends to write reports that are extremely difficult to understand and some kind of Q&A interrogation is often needed to figure out what it actually did and what the key results are. A Firecracker microVM has a process on the host that manages it. Each virtual CPU, or vCPU, has a thread in that process. Overview 

Multiple vulnerabilities have been reported in Tata Motors iRA and iRA.ev applications, which could allow an attacker to obtain sensitive credentials or access to vehicle related information and services. 

Target Audience: 
Users of the affected iRA 2.0 and iRA.ev mobile applications. 

Risk Assessment: 
Risk of unauthorized access to sensitive information, user impersonation and unauthorized modification of application data. 

Impact Assessment:
 Potential for unauthorized access and modification of data with the privileges of the impersonated user.

## 27. Closed AI models dominate the price-performance frontier

### 本文(抜粋)

Closed AI models now dominate the price-performance frontier | Village 

New research: Is vibe coding a threat to Shopify? Read the study 

Village’s LLM Atlas maps the price-performance frontier: the strongest AI model at every price point. 

Closed models hold almost every budget 

Ask Village 
 Which models are on the LLM Atlas price-performance frontier for agentic coding tasks only? Alert me when an open-weight model joins the LLM Atlas price-performance frontier. How much cheaper would DeepSeek V4.1 Flash need to be to rejoin the price-performance frontier? 

An open-weight model is the best buy at 3% of budgets. Nearly all of those are at the cheap end, held by Google’s Gemma 4 E4B and Xiaomi’s MiMo-V2.6-Flash. 

In June, it was 41%. DeepSeek V4 Flash alone was the best buy across nearly a third of budgets, with Zhipu’s GLM-5.2 just above it. 

Two OpenAI releases took most of that back. GPT-6 Luna in September and GPT-6.1 Terra in October now cover the budgets open models used to win, alongside Anthropic’s Claude Haiku 5.5. 

Open models have led on value before 

Ask Village 
 Which labs’ models have been the best buy at each budget since 2024? How has the cost of reaching GPT-5-level capability changed since its release? 

Today’s share is the lowest we have measured. At the end of every quarter since 2024, open models held at least 6% of budgets. 

The peaks came from single releases. DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp was the best buy at nearly half of all budgets the week it launched in September 2025. 

None of them lasted. After each peak, the open share fell by about half or more by the next quarter as closed labs shipped cheaper tiers. 

Open models win on value in bursts: a strong, cheap release, usually from DeepSeek, takes a wide band of budgets until the next closed model undercuts it. The latest closed releases have been both cheaper and stronger than anything open, so the band has shrunk to the very bottom of the price range. 

Why closed labs may be winning on value 

Three explanations fit the pattern: 

Closed labs can distill their own frontier models. A lab with the strongest model can train cheaper ones on its outputs in-house, and today’s cheap closed tiers followed their flagships within weeks, as Claude Haiku 5.5 did two weeks after Claude Opus 5.5. Open labs have to start from their own, weaker models, since closed labs’ terms generally bar training competitors on their outputs. 

Closed labs release whole families. OpenAI’s GPT-6 and GPT-6.1 models, from Luna and Terra up to Sol and Astra, hold 11 of the frontier’s 21 points between them, so one generation covers nearly every price. 

Closed labs may serve more cheaply. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google run inference at enormous scale, Google on its own chips, which can lower what each task costs; most open-model labs serve far less traffic. 

How we built this 

This study uses Village’s LLM Atlas , which estimates a capability score for each model and reasoning setting from public benchmark results and, with a separate cost model, its cost per task from evaluators’ runs, described in its methodology . Costs are relative to GPT-5 at high effort. For each date, we take every model released by then and find the most capable one at each of 201 budgets spread evenly on a log scale from 1% to three times GPT-5’s cost. The scores are from October 31, 2026; Atlas updates as new results arrive. 

Costs reflect what evaluators paid through hosted APIs, and open models can be cheaper to run on your own hardware. Restricting to models scored on at least 10 benchmark families, or narrowing the budgets to 3% to one times GPT-5’s cost, gives the same pattern; widening them to 0.3% to ten times raises today’s share to 16%, because tiny open models still win at the very cheapest end.

Abstract: Memory enables large language models (LLMs) to use information beyond their parameters, such as past user conversations, long documents, or an agent's earlier actions. For a frozen LLM, access to this accumulated knowledge is limited to what a memory system places in its context window. However, gathering and combining relevant information needed for each query while keeping the model's context short remains challenging for existing systems. Here we introduce Memorilla, a memory system that gathers information from an entire document collection into a fixed number of query-dependent 'memory tokens' for a frozen LLM. Memorilla encodes documents (such as user chats, book chapters) once into reusable embeddings, then learns to retrieve and place query-specific information in the LLM's input embedding space. Using only 16 memory tokens, Memorilla achieves the best performance against baselines including Mem0 and MemoRAG on five of eight benchmarks spanning personalization, long-document and knowledge-intensive question answering. Notably, on FactKG, it reaches 84.33% accuracy versus 80.43% with full-context inference, while using 108x fewer tokens to represent the source documents. Furthermore, Memorilla can facilitate continual learning in agentic systems where the document count (the agent's past turns) increases throughout the trajectory. In a TextWorld environment, we show that Memorilla reduces the agent's tendency to wander between rooms it has already explored, and improves its average success rate from 18.6% with full-history in-context learning to 24.2%. Overall, Memorilla aids frozen LLMs in accumulating and using knowledge effectively across long-context interactions and takes a step towards building continually learning AI systems.
