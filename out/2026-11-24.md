## 7. datasette-charts 0.1a0

- 出どころ: 信頼レーン: サイモン・ウィリソン（2026-11-23）
- リンク: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/23/datasette-charts/

### 本文(抜粋)

Release: datasette-charts 0.1a0 

I've been thinking about this problem for over a year now, but on Sunday I finally fired up Claude Fable 5.2 to build an initial prototype. The prototype went so well that this is now a full plugin. 

You can try it out in Datasette Lite here . It looks like this: 

The new charts plugin works against any table or arbitrary SQL query in Datasette. It defines several different chart types (using vendored D3 , Observable Plot , and topojson-client ), each with a set of heuristics for identifying when they might be a good match for a specific set of rows. 

Those suggestion thumbnails at the top are actual visualizations driven by the data, and selecting one pulls up the full chart along with tools for modifying it. You can also manually construct a chart from scratch. 

As with other Datasette visualization plugins, the current state of your chart is persisted in a #fragment in the URL - so you can bookmark charts or share them with others. 

The most important feature is that the charts take all rows into account . Previous charting plugins like datasette-vega would only show charts for the current page of results, generally up to 100 rows. 

The new plugin compiles each chart to a SQL query that aggregates across the entire result set (potentially millions of rows) using GROUP BY, time bucketing, and window functions. Each query returns a result capped at 5,000 rows, suitable for display on the chart. The plugin uses sampling to help suggest charts for larger tables without evaluating the full result set. 

I'm really happy with this initial release of the plugin. It's still very much an alpha, but it's the closest I've got to the dream of Datasette helping visualize data with as little required direction from the user as possible.

Deploying robots in real-world settings requires reliable perception throughout an interaction. To pick something up, a robot needs to follow the positions of its gripper and the object as they move. In a laboratory, a robot handling a pipette must keep track of its tip while aligning it with the opening of a tube. These tasks become harder when nearby objects clutter the view, the gripper obscures the object being handled, or the viewing angle changes. 

One way to help robots follow these interactions is to track specific locations on the objects they handle. This is the idea behind point tracking . Given a location in one video frame, a tracker estimates where that same point appears in subsequent frames, when it is occluded, and where it reappears. These trajectories can help robots learn from demonstrations, plan and guide movements, and model how objects respond to actions. 

Robotics systems often rely on off-the-shelf trackers to automatically annotate motion in videos and provide feedback during execution. And current trackers score well on established robotics benchmarks, suggesting they could be ready for wider use in real-world systems. We wanted to see whether those results hold up across the varied scenes, viewpoints, and object interactions robots encounter in practice. 

We introduce RoboTrack , a benchmark and training data suite for point tracking in robotic manipulation. RoboTrack-Bench contains 307 human-annotated videos from DROID, Ego-Exo4D, MolmoAct 2 , and EgoVerse, covering different robots and human hands, cluttered scenes, and first- and third-person viewpoints. To study how training data affects tracking in these settings, we also extend MolmoSpaces , Ai2’s simulation ecosystem for robot learning, to generate dense point tracks from simulated robot manipulation trajectories. This pipeline produces RoboTrack-15M , a new synthetic training dataset targeted toward robotic manipulation. 

RoboTrack is a benchmark and training data suite for point tracking in robotic manipulation. Built on MolmoSpaces, RoboTrack-15M (top-left) generates point tracks for training. Training with this simulated data improves tracking on real manipulation footage. RoboTrack-Bench (top-right) provides human-annotated point tracks in real manipulation videos. 

Our evaluations show that leading trackers score much lower on RoboTrack-Bench than on existing robotics benchmarks. Adding RoboTrack-15M to an existing tracker’s training improves performance on real manipulation video, with gains extending to other tracking benchmarks. We bring these pieces together on a real robot, using the resulting tracker to help guide pipette handling in wet-lab tasks. 

Today, we’re releasing both RoboTrack components and two new point tracking models: RoboTracker and RoboTracker-Warp . RoboTracker is a fine-tuned AllTracker model using our techniques and data, while RoboTracker-Warp is trained from CoWTracker. Both models improve average AJ across the three robotics benchmarks compared with their respective released baselines, and RoboTracker-Warp achieves state-of-the-art performance on four out of the five benchmarks we evaluated. We’re also releasing our paper describing the work and evaluation results.

Overview

AI evaluations help shape which systems people build, adopt, and trust. An evaluation gives a system tasks, checks its answers or actions, and produces a score. Building and maintaining a useful evaluation therefore takes real work: defining tasks that reflect domain expertise, scoring them reliably, and checking that the results support the claims made. When methods, code, and data stay private, that work is harder for others to scrutinize or build on. 

Open source offers a means to build stronger, more frequently updated, and more comparable evaluations and the infrastructure around them. Existing tasks, environments, and tools give builders a starting point, so their effort can go to domain expertise and validation. Sharing how an evaluation was built, including what went wrong, lets others find errors, improve it, and reuse it. 

The Hugging Face hub is the home for AI evaluation: we host around 3500 eval leaderboards and have seen about a 16X growth in eval datasets on the hub in the past year , to about 17000 datasets. In recent months, we have also incorporated support for agentic abstractions that are relevant to evaluation. For example, Traces that store an agent’s work in an easily reviewable and sharable way; Benchmarks that highlight datasets used to evaluate models with leaderboards; and Environments that show datasets used as agentic RL or evaluation environments. 

This guide covers four things: 

Three eras of evaluation (section 1). Evaluations moved from checking answers, to checking what an agent did, to checking what a team of agents produces. Each step adds software that can change the score. 

The steps in an agentic evaluation (section 2). We follow one task through eight steps, from the claim to the reported score. The full walkthrough, with commands, is in Appendix A . 

When a benchmark is wrong (section 3). Errors in tasks and graders are common. We look at how they are found and corrected. 

What to open strategically and why (section 4). A table sets out, component by component, what to share and what can stay protected. 

In the research community, AI evaluation terminology is notoriously inconsistent . The glossary at the end defines each term we use and notes the other names you may come across. 

1. Introduction: how evaluation design has changed

AI evaluation design has evolved in three forms as language models have become more capable and able to perform more complex work: question answer, agentic, and teams or swarms. Question-answer benchmarks such as MMLU (2020) and GPQA (2023) measure knowledge and reasoning through the answers models produce. As models became capable of planning and using tools, agentic benchmarks such as SWE-bench and WebArena expanded the scope to resolving software issues and completing tasks on websites. These tasks require evaluation setups that account for the environment in which the system operates and the changes it makes. 

Lately, research on multi-agent systems has started to add another dimension: how agents coordinate and what they accomplish together. This includes decentralized swarms , teams managed by an orchestrator , and specialized agents collaborating on scientific work . Figure 1 shows a rough timeline of these three eras of evaluations.

VP ≠ VNP over every field 

For every field, circuits for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, and for the permanent outside characteristic 2, need more than m^(c (log m)^(1/3)) gates for all large m. The result was discovered by an internal research Claude model. This folder holds the paper and its Lean 4 formalization. Both were written by AI models, and no human mathematician has checked the proof.

The text version of the screen was built decades ago, for people who couldn’t see it. What's so hard about clicking a button? 

A person clicks a button by moving the mouse to it. A model, on the other hand, looks at a screenshot and has to write the coordinates out as text (e.g., ‘x=523, y=217’) before clicking on it. Getting these numbers off by a few pixels can mean it clicks the wrong thing.

Abstract: We uncover a latent capacity for introspection in a Qwen 32B model, demonstrating that the model can detect when concepts have been injected into its earlier context and identify which concept was injected. While the model denies injection in sampled outputs, logit lens analysis reveals clear detection signals in the residual stream, which are attenuated in the final layers. Furthermore, prompting the model with accurate information about AI introspection mechanisms can dramatically strengthen this effect: the sensitivity to injection increases massively (0.3% -> 39.9%) with only a 0.6% increase in false positives. Also, mutual information between nine injected and recovered concepts rises from 0.61 bits to 1.05 bits, ruling out generic noise explanations. Our results demonstrate models can have a surprising capacity for introspection and steering awareness that is easy to overlook, with consequences for latent reasoning and safety.
