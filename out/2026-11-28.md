Thanks to our partnership with AWS, we were lucky enough to get access to a Trainium3 machine ( trn3.48xlarge , 64 NeuronCores) for a month. We spent most of the month testing Hugging Face models on it, and getting more of them to work. 

With five days left, I allowed myself a little fun. When you have a Ferrari, every now and then you want to take it out on the track. So I asked myself: what if I tried to generate videos? 

I picked LTX-2.5 from Lightricks, which generates video and sound together. By the end, once the models were loaded, a 10-second clip with sound took 22 to 23 s to generate, on half of the machine. Here is the result. 

A seal dreams of warm sand. Generated entirely on Trainium3. 

Two goals

I had two goals. The first was to see how easy it is to generate videos with diffusers on Trainium3. Not the simple clips from the examples: I wanted to make short films from a script, closer to a real use case, like drafting a movie. The second was performance. I wanted to get close to what you get with CUDA, or with LTX's own API. 

For CUDA, I found no real figure to compare against. The only one is LTX's claim of 6.8 s for a 10-second 720p clip on four B200 GPUs, with no detail on how it was measured. So I set CUDA aside and compared with LTX's online API instead, which LTX says makes a 10-second 1080p clip in 23.7 s. In the end, the setup matches it, and even beats it by about a second: 22 to 23 s for a 10-second clip at 2048×1024, once the models are loaded, on half of the machine. Not bad, considering how little time I had to optimize the pipelines. 

How it works

The input is a film: a JSON list of shots, each with a name and a prompt, much like a storyboard. LTX-2.5 does better with long prompts, so each prompt is a paragraph on the style, the subject, the motion, the camera, the light and the sound. Here is the seal film's file, with the prompts shortened: 

[ 
 { "name" : "dream" , "prompt" : "Style: cinematic, realistic, shallow depth of field. A plump seal ... is fast asleep on warm golden sand under palm trees ..." , 
 "seed" : 3 } , 
 { "name" : "nap" , "prompt" : "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... a plump grey seal is fast asleep on a small ice floe ..." , 
 "seed" : 1 , "to" : { "shot" : "iceberg" , "frames" : 9 } } , 
 { "name" : "iceberg" , "prompt" : "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... the whole iceberg slowly rolls over ... and washes over the seal. ..." } , 
 { "name" : "shake" , "prompt" : "Style: cinematic, realistic, wide-angle lens. ... It slowly shakes its head and body ... and flops down on its belly on the floe, ..." , 
 "from" : { "shot" : "iceberg" , "frames" : 25 } } 
 ] 

Each shot has a name and a prompt , and can have a seed , to keep the take I picked among several. Shots are chained with two keys. This chaining is the point, since prompts alone do not keep a subject from one shot to the next: 

from : the shot starts from the last frames of an earlier shot. Starting from the earlier shot's last frame keeps a subject's look across a cut; starting from its last 25 frames (1 s) also continues the motion. 

to : the shot ends on the first frames of a later shot, so that it leads into it. 

A shot can also be trimmed, or have a caption drawn over it. 

Making a film from shots works like animation, which has two ways to go. Straight ahead, each shot continues the one before. Pose to pose, you make the key shot first, until you get one you like; then the shots before it end on its first frames ( to ), and the shots after it start from its last frames ( from ). I wrote this book between 2011 and 2015. At the time, I expected AGI systems based on neural networks to be many decades away, but to bring remarkable benefits for humanity. I also expected significant challenges, but believed it likely that, as with other technologies, those challenges would be addressed as the capabilities were developed. 

My views have changed. With the extraordinarily reckless behaviour of the frontier labs, the systems have raced ahead much faster than I expected 1 , giving humanity far too little time to meet the challenges. Although the systems genuinely will bring — indeed, already are bringing — many wonders, they also pose a significant risk of catastrophe. The frontier labs are knowingly endangering all of humanity. What follows are two brief remarks: one on the nature of the threat posed by ASI, and another on the ethical justification for working on frontier AI. 

The nature of the threat 

The most common argument for AI existential risk, developed by Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, is loss of control of the future to advanced AI systems 2 . I believe this concern should be understood as one instance of a more fundamental issue. That issue is whether we will discover technologies latent in nature which are easy and inexpensive to develop, catastrophic in outcome, and beyond our capacity to defend against. If such “progress” in technology were happening slowly, our social institutions might successfully develop defences. But it seems likely that ASI will cause a sudden explosion of such destructive technologies, with insufficient time to adapt. 

A historical example to help prime intuition is fire. It is easy to make and extremely difficult to defend against. We have a one-cent technology, the matchstick, which requires hundreds of billions of dollars each year to defend against, as well as a whole apparatus of social institutions, technology, infrastructure and law 3 . AI systems will enable technologies which are far more destructive than matches. Understandably, people skeptical of catastrophic risks want these supposed threats spelled out in detail. This is intrinsically difficult, because we can't reliably anticipate future science and technology. While it is common to speculate about biothreats, I expect the worst threats will be technologies we haven't yet imagined. Nature is, after all, more imaginative than we. 

Now suppose that ASI enables a 100-times speed-up in technological progress, without a matching change in the rate of social progress or social defenses.  Dear Soapboxers,

AI is scaring me, and not for the reason you think. Yes, the threat of increasing hacks that could melt down the global economy or trigger nuclear Armageddon is terrifying...but I can't do sh*t about that.

Think of a traditional deep learning model like a giant game of "Telephone": data passes through layer after layer, with each layer changing the information slightly. If a network has dozens or hundreds of layers, the original learning signal can become severely degraded or lost by the time it reaches all layers, which is known as the vanishing gradient problem in AI. 
To overcome this challenge and enable the training of networks with dozens or hundreds of layers, transformers rely heavily on residual connections , also known as “skip” connections, which act like an information highway or a shortcut. Instead of forcing the data to pass exclusively through the complicated layer, a duplicate copy of the original input skips right past this layer and is added directly to its output. This ensures that the model always has the original raw data to refer back to. 
The standard residual connection implements a simple operation: point-wise summation of a layer's output with its input. While computationally efficient, this simple process has a key limitation: it treats all prior representations with equal weight, leading to information dilution in deep networks. As features propagate through many layers, specific, high-resolution details from earlier layers can be averaged out or distorted, making it difficult for the network to selectively retrieve them. This forces the network to recompute them, reducing learning efficiency. 
In our ICML 2025 paper, "DeepCrossAttention: Supercharging Transformer Residual Connections ," we studied the shortcomings of the traditional residual architecture and introduced DeepCrossAttention (DCA), which generalizes residual connections by allowing each layer to dynamically select and route features from the history of preceding layers. By allowing the model to dynamically choose which previous layers are most important at any given moment, DCA achieves comparable or improved learning, while reducing the number of parameters needed by up to 30-40%. 

How it works 

Instead of treating skip connections as a rigid, additive pipeline, DCA reframes residual learning as a dynamic routing problem. Rather than passing all prior layers forward with equal weight, DCA introduces a lightweight, layer-wise module that decides how to best combine previous embeddings on a per-token basis.

While a standard residual network uses rigid, additive point-wise connections with equal weighting, DCA introduces dynamic, learned routing weights (W) that allow each layer to selectively route and combine features from across the history of preceding layers.

My 16-year-old son recently obtained his learner’s permit and is starting to have ideas about what he wants behind the wheel. In our driveway, we have a 2023 Fisker Ocean, which is an all-electric SUV with a solar panel on the roof, an enormous rotating center touch screen, and the ability to roll down all the windows and open the sunroof at the flip of a switch. There’s also a 2005 Honda Element, which can do none of those things. If it weren’t for an aftermarket audio unit, it wouldn’t even have Bluetooth capability. 

My son always goes for the Element. In fact, he has elaborate plans for tricking it out down the road, with things like a wooden plank for sleeping on campouts. I should point out that he is part of what is supposed to be the first truly digitally native generation, yet his contempt for in-vehicle tech is something of a badge of honor.
