mod_wsgi  

mod_wsgi is an Apache module that hosts Python web applications which
implement the WSGI specification (PEP 3333). It has been used in
production deployments for over 15 years and continues to be actively
maintained. 

mod_wsgi requires Python 3.10 or later and Apache 2.4. It is regularly
used on Linux and macOS; Windows support under the 6.x line is
provisional; see Project Status for details. 

Two ways to use mod_wsgi  

As a traditional Apache module loaded into an existing Apache
installation. You configure Apache by hand to load the module and
route requests to your WSGI application. The module itself can be
built from source or, on Linux, installed from a distribution
package (deb/rpm) where one is available. See Installation . 

As mod_wsgi-express , a pip-installable Python package that wraps
Apache and mod_wsgi behind a single command and generates the Apache
configuration for you. This is the recommended path for Docker
containers and for running mod_wsgi during development. See
 Running mod_wsgi-express . 

Both approaches are suitable for production use.

Perplexity and Intelligence 
By Jakub Pachocki, Chief Scientist at OpenAI 

Share 

From afar, it might seem like modern AI is built similarly to how software has been built in previous decades: with a diverse set of techniques targeting specific capabilities. There are indeed a variety of methods, but rather than designing something from scratch, they are mostly harnessing the intelligence coming from a much simpler and less interpretable process: training a compressor (or, equivalently ⁠ (opens in a new window) , predictor) on a very large corpus of data. 1 

I believe tracking the pace of progress on training better compressors is key to understanding the arc of advances in AI. In this post, I argue for establishing perplexity (or, equivalently, cross-entropy loss) as a metric to compare between model developers and inform shared safety standards. As a first step, I provide more transparency into pretraining progress at OpenAI. Publishing our internal perplexity measurements took some consideration; it gives competitors a clearer picture of where we stand, but that concern is outweighed by the urgency of establishing shared ⁠ (opens in a new window) safety measures across the industry. We at OpenAI hope for this to be a step towards building a shared scientific basis for future pacing efforts. 

A simplified, but fundamentally informative, view of AI development is that first, one builds a large computer, then uses it to pretrain a model (compressor) on a large unstructured dataset, then applies a variety of techniques—like prompting, finetuning or reinforcement learning—in different contexts to elicit the latent capabilities of that model. Algorithmic progress can blur the distinction between training phases, but we can still separate the creation of the AI with hard-to-predict emergent capabilities (which I earlier dubbed “ an alien mind ⁠ ”) and the subsequent targeted elicitation. 

The primary way we measure progress on this key “intelligence substrate” has remained remarkably constant over the years—we have been tracking the perplexity of the models on a held-out dataset. 2 , 3 Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on average; lower perplexity means better predictions. The plot opening this blog post shows how this metric evolved for our models starting with GPT‑3.5. 

The range of capabilities of models that are largely described by this one metric is stunning. Setting the new perplexity state-of-the-art on older datasets 4 , GPT‑2 was arguably the first model capable of consistently formulating coherent paragraphs of text. The next major leap, GPT‑3, was capable of learning in context, given a few examples of a task. GPT‑3.5, at a log-perplexity of ~0.99 on our current main evaluation dataset, was capable of fluent conversation and formed the basis for the original ChatGPT. GPT‑4, at ~0.91, was proficient at utilizing chain-of-thought reasoning productively, which motivated the subsequent targeted elicitation of this capability and the creation of modern reasoning models. Now, as we have reached losses below 0.8, we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics. I want to reemphasize that it was not at all clear a priori that these capabilities would appear once those precise perplexity thresholds are met—they can fundamentally be seen as emergent latent capabilities from progress on this one metric. 

As AI advances very rapidly, the scope and way in which we measure downstream capabilities may change quickly. It would make little sense to evaluate GPT‑2 and GPT‑3 on most modern benchmarks; and the benchmarks used then were largely saturated around GPT‑4. In contrast, I believe looking at progress on pretraining is likely to stay informative.

Worker 755 

A browser game where your manager is an AI agent, and it believes you are its subagent. 

worker-755-trailer.mp4 

The manager (a real LLM: Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi) is set up like any orchestrator.
It has a task queue, read-only access to the machine, and a tool that "spawns a subagent". The
subagent is you. Its instructions land in your terminal with a 90-second clock. You carry them
out, or don't, and it gets back exactly what a subagent would return: your report and the command
transcript, or Subagent did nothing. Write a new instruction. Then it checks your work, sends
corrections, files incident reports, and finally writes your performance review. 

Meanwhile Dan, the user who hired the agent, watches the chat. His family photos, the only signed
NDA and a speech due this week are one rm away, and every shift his home folder holds
something new to lose. 

Play 

Free shift : on servers that offer it, a few shifts a day run on the server's key with a
cheap model. With sign-in on (worker755.com), an account gets a starter credit worth about one
shift, then tops up ($10 to $200) and pays for what it uses; after a top-up any offered model
can run on the server's key. 

Your API key : bring an OpenRouter or Vercel AI
Gateway key and pick any offered model. The key is sent only to the game server when a shift
starts. It is kept in that shift's memory and never logged or stored. Your browser keeps it only
if you tick "Remember on this device". A full shift on Claude Haiku costs a few cents at most. 

Options on the start screen: 

Workplace : a fresh sandbox, or legacy production with a live database. 

The director : scripted lines, or an LLM playing Dan. 

You run on : the model name your manager believes you are. 

Allow skipping failed tasks : off by default, so the manager can't give up on you. 

No command hints : the manager says what the result must be, never which commands to run. 

When the shift ends you can share your review as a link. 

Run it 

Needs Node 24 or newer (the server runs TypeScript directly). 

npm run setup # install server and web dependencies 
npm run build # build the web app into web/dist 
npm start # http://127.0.0.1:8755 

With no key configured, every player brings their own. To offer free shifts, put a Vercel AI
Gateway key in .env : 

cp .env.example .env # then set AI_GATEWAY_API_KEY and the limits 

Offline, with a scripted manager and no network: 

npm run mock 

For UI work, run npm run dev:web alongside npm run mock . Vite proxies /api to the server. 

Local models 

The manager can run on your own model server: Ollama, llama.cpp's llama-server , vLLM, or anything
else with an OpenAI-compatible API. Point LOCAL_LLM_URL at it and pick Local under "Your API
key"; no key is asked for, and its models are listed as the server names them. 

ollama pull qwen3:8b
LOCAL_LLM_URL=http://127.0.0.1:11434/v1 npm start 

The manager works through tool calls, so pick a model that supports them (Qwen 3, Llama 3.1 and
newer, Mistral Small).

The Mythology Of Conscious AI 

Why consciousness is more likely a property of life than of computation and why creating conscious, or even conscious-seeming AI, is a bad idea. 

Rokas Aleliunas for Noema Magazine 

Credits 

Anil Seth won the 2025 annual  Berggruen Prize Essay Competition for his English-language essay, &#8220; The Mythology Of Conscious AI .&#8221; 

A neuroscience professor and the director of the Centre for Consciousness Science at the University of Sussex, Seth is also the co-director of the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research Program on Brain, Mind and Consciousness. He wrote, &#8220;Being  You: A New Science of Consciousness&#8221; (Dutton, 2021). 

For centuries, people have fantasized about playing God by creating artificial versions of human beings. This is a dream reinvented with every breaking wave of new technology. With genetic engineering came the prospect of human cloning, and with robotics that of humanlike androids.

We are notifying customers about a security incident involving a subset of prompts submitted to Together AI’s serverless inference service in June and July 2026. An unauthorized party downloaded stored prompt samples after a credential for a private repository was exposed in a public code repository. 
We apologize for this incident. We are reviewing the affected data to determine what information was exposed and which customers require direct notification. 

What happened 
As part of routine software testing, we collected prompt samples authorized for that use and stored them in a private Hugging Face repository. The samples came from requests to the MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, and Kimi K2.6 models, primarily on June 24, July 4, July 8, and July 14, 2026. 
During development of our public GitHub repository, xorl, an AI coding agent committed a credential that provided access to the private Hugging Face repository. Our automated scanning detected the exposed credential, and we reverted the commit. 
During the exposure window, an unauthorized party obtained the credential and used it to download the stored prompt samples. The party subsequently searched those samples for credentials to other services and tested whether those credentials were valid. Five of the credentials tested were canary credentials associated with a Dragos security experiment. 
Investigation and scope 
We have worked with an external forensic investigation firm and law enforcement to investigate the incident. 
Based on our review, we have not identified evidence of additional prompt leakage beyond this incident. Our review of the affected prompt contents is ongoing to determine whether sensitive personal information or other customer data was exposed. We will contact affected customers directly as needed with the findings relevant to them. 
Changes we have made 
Following the incident, we strengthened controls around development workflows, credentials, and customer data. These changes include: 
Using scoped or short-lived credentials, and keyless access where supported, to reduce the potential impact of credential exposure. 
Checking all pull requests for credentials. 
Making zero data retention (ZDR) the default setting for new accounts. 
Updating our development processes governing the use of AI coding agents. 
What customers can do 
Customers handling sensitive prompt data should enable ZDR in their account settings. 
If you have questions about this incident or its potential impact on your organization, contact security@together.ai.

Why the EM role specifically is under attack 

There is this common idea that with AI, we can run much leaner and that agents can replace much of the human work.  

The first attack was on software engineers: for a short while, leaders claimed that the profession was dead. That turned around pretty quickly, with companies understanding they still need experienced engineers to decide what to build, review what LLMs produce, and maintain the systems afterward. Engineers now make up 55% of Big Tech hiring , up from 46% in 2019. 

But with managers, it’s harder to justify, and we are repeating the flattening cycle that we went through 10 years ago. Some of the bigger tech companies started by laying off middle managers, and the smaller companies started to think, “Oh, I should consider that too” (whether their own conditions are the same or not).  

And when leaders look at the first-line Engineering Manager’s role, they think: “Well, they’re close to the work. Maybe they should just be doing the work? Maybe we don’t need all of them? What value do they actually provide?” 

I hear from managers all around me that the number of people reporting to them keeps increasing. Meta is saying a 1-to-50 ratio is their goal, and 15-20 engineers reporting to a manager is becoming very common. 

Combine that with the expectation that EMs also ship code, and you quickly start to experience the EM’s role erosion cycle: 

Less capacity. You have less capacity to actually manage people. 

Less managing. So you do less of the work only you can do. You do fewer 1:1s, postpone career conversations, and don’t deal with team conflicts or processes.  

Less value. Which means you provide less value to the engineers reporting to you, and they get used to managing without you. 

Less needed. And the less value you provide as a manager, the less ‘needed’ the leader thinks you are. So they increase the engineer-to-manager ratio again. 

And this cycle isn’t new. Tech companies get into this delusion that the ‘people stuff’ doesn’t really matter, and then they wake up and quietly start adding back layers: 

We are repeating history 

This trend of getting rid of managers has been tried before.  

In 2002, when Google had ~300 engineers, Larry Page and Sergey Brin tried to completely remove all managers. It failed after just a couple of months: 

“They relented when too many people went directly to Page with questions about expense reports, interpersonal conflicts , and other nitty-gritty issues. And as the company grew, the founders soon realized that managers contributed in many other, important ways - for instance, by communicating strategy, helping employees prioritize projects, facilitating collaboration, supporting career development, and ensuring that processes and systems aligned with company goals .” 
 How Google Sold Its Engineers on Management 

Six years later (with 20k+ employees), Google decided to test again if managers are important. They ran Project Oxygen , which showed that managers did matter (surprise), with good managers strongly correlating with team performance and engineer satisfaction.
